When I first read the A2 brief, the most important thing that stood out to me was that this project is not about building a complete social platform from scratch. Instead, the goal is to prototype the unique feature that gives a specific Bla+Bla community a reason to exist on this platform rather than somewhere more generic. That immediately changed how I thought about the task. Instead of asking “what features should this app have?”, the more useful question became “what is the one thing this community needs that is currently unsupported, inconvenient, or too general on other platforms?”

Our project is currently focused on [insert your community/topic here]. At a surface level, it would be easy to imagine many possible features for this kind of space, such as messaging, posting, commenting, profiles, or media sharing. However, these are mostly baseline platform behaviours rather than the distinctive value of our prototype. The brief makes it clear that standard services such as authentication are already handled by Bla+Bla, and that everyday social functions only matter if they are tightly integrated into the concept. This means our job is to identify the specific interaction or support mechanism that genuinely responds to our users’ needs.

A feature may look interesting on paper, but if it does not address a meaningful user need, it becomes difficult to justify both conceptually and technically.

Based on this, I have started separating essential functionality from optional functionality. The essential functionality is whatever directly enables users to complete the main purpose of the community. In our case, that means users need to be able to [insert the one key user action here]. Without this, the prototype would fail to demonstrate its core value. Optional functionality includes anything that may improve the experience but is not necessary for proving the concept, such as [insert examples relevant to your project]. Making this distinction early is important because it helps control scope and prevents the project from drifting toward a full-featured platform imitation.

Feasibility is also shaping my thinking at this stage. Since this is a prototype built within a limited timeframe and a specific full-stack structure, I need to focus on an idea that is technically meaningful but still realistic to implement well. A smaller feature with a clear purpose, coherent interaction flow, and stable execution is likely to be stronger than a broader concept that tries to do too much and ends up underdeveloped. At this early stage, I am not treating the prototype as a list of pages or screens. I am treating it as a response to a need. That shift is useful because it keeps later design decisions grounded in purpose rather than aesthetics or convenience. In the next stage, I want to compare a few possible ways this feature could work in practice, especially in terms of user flow, required data, and technical complexity. That will help me move from a broad concept into a more concrete and justifiable design direction.
